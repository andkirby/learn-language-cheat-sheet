Nouns

Gender and number belong to the noun.

Noun gender & plural

A1forms & use

Learn each noun with its article: grammatical gender is not biological sex. Plural usually adds -s.

  • un livre → des livres — a book → books; masculine
  • une table → des tables — a table → tables; feminine
  • un pays → des pays — a country → countries; final -s unchanged
  • un cheval → des chevaux — a horse → horses; common -al change
Les petites tables sont ici.
The small tables are here. The article and adjective match the feminine plural noun.
Endings are clues, not guarantees: learn gender and irregular plurals with the noun. Not all -al or -ou words change to -aux/-oux.

Articles & determiners

Choose a meaning, then match the noun.

A, the — or no article?

A1forms & use

French usually needs an article even when English uses none. Choose specific, general or one unspecified member.

  • le / la / les — the; also a whole category, as in les chats — cats in general
  • l' — le/la before a vowel or silent h
  • un / une / des — a masculine / a feminine / some plural items
J'aime les chats. J'ai un chat.
I like cats. I have a cat.
Generic likes use a definite article. An unmodified profession after être normally has no article; negated indefinite articles usually become de, with exceptions.

An amount: du, de la, de l’

A1forms & use

Use a partitive for an unspecified amount of something treated as a mass. A definite article can instead name the category.

  • du pain — some bread; masculine mass noun
  • de la soupe — some soup; feminine
  • de l'eau — some water; vowel
  • pas de pain — no bread; ordinary negative amount
J'aime le café, mais je bois du thé.
I like coffee, but I drink tea.
Negative amounts usually use de/d’. After être or in an explicit contrast, the article can remain. du may also be de + le, not an amount.

How much? How many?

A1forms & use

A number directly counts a noun. A measure or common quantity phrase usually links to its noun with de/d’.

  • deux pommes — two apples; no de after the simple number
  • beaucoup de pommes — a lot of apples
  • un litre d'eau — a litre of water
  • autant de livres que toi — as many books as you
  • à dix kilomètres / moins 10 % — ten kilometres away / ten percent off
J'ai trois pommes, mais elle en a autant que moi.
I have three apples, but she has as many as I do.
de can combine with a definite article for a specific set: beaucoup des invités = many of the guests. Not every quantity construction is bare de.

My, your, his or her?

A1forms & use

Choose the owner first, then match the possessed noun’s gender and number. son/sa can mean his or her.

  • mon / ma / mes — my: masculine singular / feminine singular / plural
  • ton / ta / tes — your: familiar singular owner
  • son / sa / ses — his/her/its: noun decides the form
  • notre / nos ; votre / vos — our singular/plural item; your polite/plural owner
  • leur / leurs — their singular/plural item
Paul aime sa sœur. Marie aime son frère.
Paul loves his sister. Marie loves her brother. The possessed noun decides sa/son.
Use mon/ton/son before a feminine noun beginning with a vowel or silent h: mon amie. It does not make the noun masculine.

This or that + noun

A1forms & use

French demonstrative determiners agree with the noun. The same forms can mean this or that; add a suffix for a contrast.

  • ce livre — this/that book; masculine
  • cet hôtel — this/that hotel; masculine before vowel or silent h
  • cette maison — this/that house; feminine
  • ces livres / ces maisons — these/those books / houses
Je préfère ce livre-ci à ce livre-là.
I prefer this book to that book.
cet is still masculine. ce/cet/cette/ces need a noun; use celui/celle for “this one”. h aspiré keeps ce, as in ce héros.

All, every, some + noun

A2forms & use

A determiner accompanies a noun. Match tout to the noun’s gender/number; chaque introduces a singular noun.

  • tout le livre / toute la journée — the whole book / the whole day; masculine / feminine noun frame
  • tous les jours / toutes les semaines — every day / every week
  • chaque jour — each/every day; singular
  • quelques livres / certains livres — a few books / some particular books
Tous mes amis viennent, mais certains voisins travaillent.
All my friends are coming, but some neighbours are working.
Not all English “all” phrases use the same structure. chaque is singular; do not use it as the standalone pronoun “each one”.

Adjectives & comparison

Endings, position and degree.

Adjective endings

A1forms & use

An adjective normally agrees with the noun it describes. Start from the masculine singular form, but learn irregular patterns.

  • petit / petite / petits / petites — small: masculine / feminine / masculine plural / feminine plural
  • rouge → rouge / rouges — red: final -e already present
  • bon → bonne ; heureux → heureuse — good; happy: common changed feminine forms
  • beau → belle ; nouveau → nouvelle — beautiful; new: irregular feminine forms
Les petites maisons sont blanches.
The small houses are white. Both adjectives agree with feminine plural maisons.
“Add e” is only the regular model. Some adjectives change their stem; do not change a noun’s gender to fit the adjective.

Before or after the noun?

A1forms & use

Most French adjectives follow the noun. A small frequent set usually comes before it; position can also change meaning.

  • une voiture rouge — a red car: colour normally follows
  • un petit jardin — a small garden: common pre-noun adjective
  • un bon repas — a good meal: common pre-noun adjective
  • son propre bureau / un bureau propre — his/her own office / a clean office
Mon ancien voisin habite une maison ancienne.
My former neighbour lives in an old house.
Do not use one “always before/after” mnemonic for every adjective. Position can be a meaning choice.

More, as, less — and the most

A2forms & use

Compare an adjective with plus/aussi/moins … que. For a superlative, add the appropriate definite article.

  • plus grand que / aussi grand que / moins grand que — taller than / as tall as / less tall than
  • la ville la plus calme — the quietest city; article before following adjective
  • le meilleur repas — the best meal; bon → meilleur
  • Il travaille mieux. — He works better; bien → mieux
Cette chambre est plus grande que la mienne.
This room is bigger than mine.
Adjectives agree; adverbs do not. Better is meilleur for a noun’s quality, mieux for doing something well. Amounts use de, not this adjective pattern.

Adverbs

Amount, frequency and manner.

Very, too, enough, often

A1forms & use

Choose degree for an adjective/adverb, amount for an action/noun, and frequency for how often.

  • très / trop / assez — very / too / enough; context supplies the adjective/action
  • beaucoup / peu — a lot / little, not much
  • souvent / parfois / rarement — often / sometimes / rarely
  • environ / presque / autant — approximately / almost / as much
Elle travaille beaucoup, mais elle est très calme.
She works a lot, but she is very calm.
très does not normally modify a verb by itself: use beaucoup. Add de when a quantity phrase introduces a noun.

How? Adverbs in -ment

B1forms & use

Many manner adverbs use the adjective’s feminine form + -ment. Their position depends on length, meaning and the verb construction.

  • lent → lente → lentement — slow → slowly
  • poli → poliment — polite → politely; vowel-ending masculine base
  • prudent → prudemment — careful → carefully; -ent → -emment
  • constant → constamment — constant → constantly; -ant → -amment
  • bon → bien — good → well; irregular adverb
Elle explique clairement la règle.
She explains the rule clearly.
Not every adjective forms an adverb mechanically. Do not apply one fixed position to all adverbs; long manner adverbs often follow the participle.

Numbers, dates & clock time

Closed grammatical patterns, not a vocabulary course.

Numbers, dates & clock time

A1forms & use

Use cardinals for most dates, premier for the first. Clock-time phrases have their own forms; numbers can agree in limited ways.

  • le premier mai / le deux mai — 1 May / 2 May
  • Il est une heure / deux heures. — It is one o’clock / two o’clock.
  • à huit heures et demie — at half past eight
  • vingt et un / quatre-vingts / quatre-vingt-un — 21 / 80 / 81; France default
  • premier / première ; deuxième — first masculine/feminine; second
Le cours commence le trois juin à neuf heures.
The class starts on 3 June at nine o’clock.
This is grammar, not a full number-word list. France uses soixante-dix/quatre-vingts/quatre-vingt-dix; other standard regional forms exist.

Present forms & meaning

Common patterns before exceptions.

Regular present endings

A1forms & use

A conjugated verb matches its subject. Three useful models are parler (speak), finir (finish) and vendre (sell).

  • je parle / finis / vends — I speak / finish / sell
  • tu parles / finis / vends — you speak / finish / sell; familiar singular
  • il parle / finit / vend — he speaks / finishes / sells; also elle/on endings
  • nous parlons / finissons / vendons — we speak / finish / sell
  • vous parlez / finissez / vendez — you speak / finish / sell; polite or plural
  • ils parlent / finissent / vendent — they speak / finish / sell; also elles
Nous finissons le travail et nous parlons de demain.
We finish the work and talk about tomorrow.
These are models, not rules for every infinitive with the same ending: partir is not finir. Written endings may differ even when they sound alike.

Essential irregular present

A1forms & use

Learn these very frequent verbs as small paradigms. Forms below follow I, familiar you, he/she, we, polite/plural you, they.

  • être: suis, es, est, sommes, êtes, sont — be: I am, you are, he/she is, we/you/they are
  • avoir: ai, as, a, avons, avez, ont — have: I/you have, he/she has, we/you/they have
  • aller: vais, vas, va, allons, allez, vont — go: I/you go, he/she goes, we/you/they go
  • faire: fais, fais, fait, faisons, faites, font — do/make: same person order
J'ai une question. Vous êtes prêts ?
I have a question. Are you ready? prêts assumes a masculine or mixed plural group.
Do not attach regular -er endings to these verbs. In speech and writing, je becomes j’ before a vowel: j’ai.

Doing now — or just did?

A1forms & use

French present often covers both English “do” and “am doing”. Use être en train de to emphasize an activity in progress; venir de introduces a recent completed action.

  • Je travaille. — I work / I am working; context decides
  • Je suis en train de travailler. — I am in the middle of working
  • Je viens de travailler. — I have just worked
Elle vient d'arriver. Nous sommes en train de manger.
She has just arrived. We are in the middle of eating.
Do not add en train de to every English -ing sentence. venir de + infinitive means recent past; venir de + place can mean come from.

Verb patterns

Subjects, infinitives and shared actions.

Reflexive & pronominal verbs

A1forms & use

The infinitive’s se changes with the subject and normally sits before its verb. Some verbs are reflexive in meaning; others are reciprocal or idiomatic.

  • je me / tu te / il se — I … myself / you … yourself / he … himself; also elle/on se
  • nous nous / vous vous / ils se — we … ourselves / you … yourselves / they … themselves; also elles se
  • Je m'habille. — I get dressed; vowel elision
  • Elle se lève. — She gets up; se lever is the dictionary form
Nous nous levons tôt.
We get up early.
Not every se means “oneself”. Compound forms use être, but participle agreement needs the direct/indirect-object distinction.

Can, want, must — and must not

A1forms & use

Use pouvoir, vouloir or devoir plus an infinitive. Impersonal il faut expresses necessity; it does not refer to a male person.

  • pouvoir: peux, peux, peut, pouvons, pouvez, peuvent — can/be able: I, familiar you, he/she, we, polite/plural you, they
  • vouloir: veux, veux, veut, voulons, voulez, veulent — want: same person order
  • devoir: dois, dois, doit, devons, devez, doivent — must/have to: same person order
  • il faut / il ne faut pas — it is necessary / it is necessary not to
Tu peux entrer, mais il ne faut pas courir.
You can come in, but you must not run.
il ne faut pas means must not, not “don’t need to”. These modal frames take an infinitive without inserting à/de.

While/by doing: en + -ant

B1forms & use

Use en + present participle to link a simultaneous action or manner to the same subject. Form most participles from the nous stem without -ons + -ant.

  • parler → parlant ; finir → finissant — speak → speaking; finish → finishing
  • faire → faisant ; lire → lisant — do/make → doing; read → reading
  • être → étant ; avoir → ayant ; savoir → sachant — be / have / know: three irregular participles
Elle apprend en lisant.
She learns by reading. She performs both actions.
English -ing is often a French infinitive, not a gerund. The same-subject condition is essential; independent participial clauses and perfect gerund are deferred.

Talking about the past

Form, viewpoint and agreement.

Passé composé: a past event

A1forms & use

Use the present of avoir or être plus a past participle. It can correspond to English simple past or present perfect, according to context.

  • parler → parlé ; finir → fini ; vendre → vendu — speak → spoken; finish → finished; sell → sold
  • j'ai / tu as / il a + parlé — I / you / he spoke; avoir is the auxiliary
  • nous avons / vous avez / ils ont + parlé — we / you / they spoke; elle a, elles ont too
  • fait / eu / été / pris — done/made / had / been / taken; common irregular participles
Hier, j'ai fini le livre. Je l'ai déjà lu.
Yesterday I finished the book. I have already read it.
The auxiliary is not translated word for word. Most verbs use avoir; learn être verbs and agreement separately. Avoir can agree with a preceding direct object.

Être in the compound past

A1forms & use

A bounded group of intransitive verbs uses être. With these non-pronominal verbs, the participle agrees with the subject.

  • aller → allé ; venir → venu — go → gone; come → come
  • arriver → arrivé ; partir → parti — arrive → arrived; leave → left
  • entrer → entré ; sortir → sorti — go in → gone in; go out → gone out
  • rester → resté ; tomber → tombé — stay → stayed; fall → fallen
  • naître → né ; mourir → mort — be born → born; die → died
Elles sont arrivées. Il est parti.
They [feminine] arrived. He left.
Movement alone does not select être: marcher/courir use avoir. Some verbs switch to avoir with a direct object. Reflexive agreement has separate limits.

Imparfait: background & habit

A2forms & use

Describe an ongoing past situation, state or habit without presenting its completed boundaries. Take the present nous stem, remove -ons, then add these endings.

  • je parlais / tu parlais / il parlait — I / you / he was speaking, spoke habitually
  • nous parlions / vous parliez / ils parlaient — we / you / they were speaking; elle/on and elles follow the matching endings
  • être → ét- : j’étais, nous étions — be → I was, we were; exceptional stem
Quand j’étais enfant, je lisais tous les soirs.
When I was a child, I read every evening.
Imparfait is a viewpoint, not an automatic translation of English “was” or “used to”. Completed bounded episodes can use passé composé instead.

Past story: event or background?

A2forms & use

Passé composé advances completed events; imparfait supplies setting, an unfolding activity or a habit. The whole context decides.

  • Il pleuvait quand elle est arrivée. — It was raining when she arrived: background + event
  • Elle lisait. Puis elle a fermé le livre. — She was reading. Then she closed the book.
  • Il savait. / Il a su. — He knew. / He found out; state versus change in a suitable context
Je préparais le repas quand le téléphone a sonné.
I was preparing the meal when the phone rang.
No time word guarantees one tense. The same situation can be framed differently; “long = imparfait, short = passé composé” is false.

Before another past moment

B1forms & use

The plus-que-parfait locates an action before a past reference point. Use the imperfect of the usual auxiliary plus the participle.

  • j’avais / tu avais / il avait + fini — I / you / he had finished
  • nous avions / vous aviez / ils avaient + fini — we / you / they had finished
  • elle était partie / elles étaient parties — she had left / they [feminine] had left; être agreement
Quand je suis arrivé, elle était déjà partie.
When I arrived, she had already left.
Keep the same auxiliary and agreement rules as passé composé. Not every English had-construction maps here: past ongoing duration often uses imparfait + depuis.

An earlier direct object: agreement

B1forms & use

With avoir, agree the past participle with a direct object placed before it. A following direct object or an indirect object does not trigger this agreement.

  • J’ai écrit les lettres. — I wrote the letters: direct object after the participle
  • Je les ai écrites. — I wrote them [the letters]: preceding feminine plural direct object
  • les lettres que j’ai écrites — the letters I wrote: que represents the earlier direct object
  • Je leur ai écrit. — I wrote to them: leur is indirect; no agreement
Cette photo, je l’ai prise hier.
This photo — I took it yesterday. photo is feminine; prendre → pris/prise.
Do not agree with every earlier pronoun. Reflexive se can be direct or indirect. Exceptions with following infinitives/causatives and en are outside this ordinary COD rule.

Future reference

Plans and predictions in context.

Plans & future events

A1forms & use

Present plus a future context, aller + infinitive and futur simple can all refer to the future. Choose in context; proximity alone is not a rule.

  • Je pars demain. — I leave / am leaving tomorrow
  • Je vais partir. — I’m going to leave: present aller + infinitive
  • je parlerai / tu parleras / il parlera — I / you / he will speak
  • nous parlerons / vous parlerez / ils parleront — we / you / they will speak
Quand tu arriveras, nous mangerons.
When you arrive, we will eat. French uses future in this future-time quand clause.
Futur simple uses an infinitive-based stem (-re loses final e), but common verbs have irregular stems. “Near future” can describe plans beyond the next few minutes.

Requests, commands & subjunctive

Use the construction your meaning needs.

Polite, advised or imagined

A1forms & use

Start with polite chunks; at B1 use the future stem with imperfect endings for wishes, advice and imagined outcomes.

  • je voudrais / j’aimerais — I would like; polite wish/request
  • tu devrais / on pourrait — you should / we could; advice/suggestion
  • je parlerais / tu parlerais / il parlerait — I / you / he would speak
  • nous parlerions / vous parleriez / ils parleraient — we / you / they would speak
Je voudrais un billet. Pourriez-vous m’aider ?
I’d like a ticket. Could you help me?
Use the future stem, including irregular stems, with -ais/-ais/-ait/-ions/-iez/-aient. English habitual “would” in a past story usually needs imparfait, not this mood.

Commands & invitations

A1forms & use

Use the tu, nous or vous command without a subject pronoun. Positive commands move object pronouns after the verb; negative commands keep them before it.

  • parle / parlons / parlez — speak / let’s speak / speak [polite/plural]
  • finis / finissons / finissez — finish / let’s finish / finish [polite/plural]
  • prends / prenons / prenez — take / let’s take / take [polite/plural]
  • Donne-le-moi. / Ne me le donne pas. — Give it to me. / Don’t give it to me.
Écoute-moi, s’il te plaît. Ne parle pas si vite.
Listen to me, please. Don’t speak so fast.
Most -er tu commands lose -s, but restore it before attached y/en: vas-y, parles-en. Common irregular commands must be learned.

Present subjunctive forms

B1forms & use

After a subjunctive trigger, most verbs take -e, -es, -e, -ions, -iez, -ent. A common model uses the present ils stem without -ent.

  • que je parle / tu parles / il parle — that I / you / he speak; subjunctive forms
  • que nous parlions / vous parliez / ils parlent — that we / you / they speak
  • que je finisse / nous finissions — that I finish / we finish; finir model
  • que je vienne / nous venions — that I come / we come; two stems
Il faut que nous finissions avant midi.
We need to finish before noon.
The ils-stem shortcut is not universal. Two-stem and irregular verbs need their own forms; present indicative and subjunctive can look identical.

When do I need the subjunctive?

B1forms & use

Use the bounded present-subjunctive frames for obligation, will and emotion. Que alone does not choose the mood.

  • il faut que / je veux que — it is necessary that / I want someone to: subjunctive
  • je suis content que — I am glad that: subjunctive, even about a fact
  • j’espère que / je crois que — I hope that / I believe that: affirmative statements normally take indicative
  • pour que / bien que — so that / although: subjunctive in the frames taught here
Je suis contente que tu sois ici. J’espère que tu resteras.
I’m glad you’re here. I hope you’ll stay. A feminine speaker uses contente.
Subjunctive does not simply mean “unreal”. Present forms here refer to simultaneous/future situations; anterior-event subjunctive is outside this bounded card.

Passive voice

Focus on what is affected.

Focus on what is affected

B1forms & use

The passive uses a conjugated être + past participle agreeing with the affected subject. The agent is optional, usually introduced by par.

  • La lettre est envoyée. — The letter is sent: present passive
  • La lettre a été envoyée. — The letter was/has been sent: compound-past passive
  • Les lettres ont été envoyées. — The letters were sent: feminine plural agreement
Le pont a été construit par une équipe locale.
The bridge was built by a local team.
French also often uses active subjects or on. Do not translate every English passive automatically. This card’s productive tense range is present and passé composé.

Subjects & stressed pronouns

Choose the person and the role.

Who is acting? Subject pronouns

A1forms & use

French normally needs an expressed subject. Match the verb to the pronoun; tu/vous also signal the relationship between speakers.

  • je / tu / il / elle — I / you [familiar singular] / he or it [masculine] / she or it [feminine]
  • nous / vous / ils / elles — we / you [polite singular or plural] / they [masculine/mixed] / they [feminine]
  • on + third-person singular — we in conversation; people in general; someone, depending on context
On arrive demain. Vous venez aussi ?
We’re arriving tomorrow. Are you coming too? Context identifies on and whether vous is one polite addressee or several people.
on always takes a third-person singular verb, even when it means we. It is not a plural nous form. tu versus vous depends on context and relationship.

Me, him, us: stressed pronouns

A1forms & use

Use stressed pronouns after many prepositions, for emphasis and in coordinated subjects. They are different from pre-verb object pronouns.

  • moi / toi / lui / elle — me / you [familiar singular] / him / her
  • nous / vous / eux / elles — us / you [polite/plural] / them [masculine/mixed] / them [feminine]
  • avec moi / pour elle / chez eux — with me / for her / at their place
Léa et moi, nous partons avec eux.
Léa and I are leaving with them.
A stressed pronoun does not normally replace the required subject pronoun by itself: Moi, je… . Some verb frames use à lui rather than the clitic lui.

Questions & exclamations

No English do-support.

Yes/no questions

A1forms & use

English do/does has no equivalent auxiliary here. Use statement order with question intonation, est-ce que, or formal inversion.

  • Tu viens ? — Are you coming? conversational statement order
  • Est-ce que tu viens ? — Are you coming? explicit question frame
  • Viens-tu ? — Are you coming? formal inversion
  • Pourriez-vous attendre ? — Could you wait? polite conditional chunk
Est-ce que vous avez un billet ?
Do you have a ticket?
Use est-ce qu’ before a vowel. Inversion joins verb and subject pronoun with a hyphen; do not move a noun directly after the verb as a general rule.

Who, what, where, which?

A1forms & use

Ask for the missing role. People and things have distinct subject/object question patterns; quel agrees with its noun.

  • Qui vient ? / Qui est-ce que tu vois ? — Who is coming? / Whom do you see?
  • Qu’est-ce qui change ? / Qu’est-ce que tu veux ? — What is changing? / What do you want?
  • où / quand / comment / pourquoi — where / when / how / why
  • combien de livres — how many books
  • quel / quelle / quels / quelles — which/what + masculine/feminine singular/plural noun
Quel train prends-tu ? Où est-ce qu’il va ?
Which train are you taking? Where does it go?
qui can be a people-question subject or object. For a thing-subject use qu’est-ce qui; for a thing-object use qu’est-ce que. Do not insert another subject into a subject question.

What a…! How…!

A2forms & use

Use quel + noun to exclaim about a thing, agreeing with the noun. Comme/que can introduce a fuller exclamation about degree.

  • Quel beau jardin ! — What a beautiful garden! masculine singular
  • Quelle bonne idée ! — What a good idea! feminine singular
  • Quels beaux livres ! / Quelles belles fleurs ! — What beautiful books! / What beautiful flowers!
  • Comme il fait chaud ! / Qu’il fait chaud ! — How hot it is!
Quelle belle journée ! Comme je suis contente !
What a lovely day! How happy I am! A feminine speaker uses contente.
Do not add an English-style indefinite article after quel: quelle idée, not quelle une idée. Exclamative quel follows the noun’s gender and number.

Negation & restriction

Position the negative around the right verb.

Not & never: the basic frame

A1forms & use

Standard written negation uses ne/n’ before the finite verb and pas or jamais after it. There is no English do-support.

  • Je ne pars pas. — I am not leaving / I don’t leave
  • Je ne pars jamais. — I never leave
  • Je n'ai pas réservé. — I haven’t booked; negation surrounds the auxiliary
  • Pas maintenant. — Not now; short negative answer
Elle ne va pas venir.
She is not going to come. Negation surrounds va, the conjugated verb.
Conversational French often drops ne/n’; keep it in the standard written model. An object/reflexive pronoun sits between ne and the verb.

Nothing, nobody, only, neither

A2forms & use

Replace pas with the negative word needed by your meaning. A negative word can also be the subject. Restrictive ne…que means only.

  • ne…plus / ne…rien / ne…personne — no longer / nothing / nobody
  • ne…aucun + noun — not a single + noun; aucun agrees with the noun
  • ne…ni…ni — neither…nor
  • ne…que — only; a restriction, not a negative assertion
Je ne vois personne. Personne ne vient.
I see nobody. Nobody is coming.
Do not normally add pas to these basic patterns. rien and personne have different positions in compound forms; place que before what is restricted.

Introducing & describing

Identity, description and existence differ.

C’est, il est, il y a

A1forms & use

Separate identification, description and existence. A noun phrase introduced with c’est differs from an adjective describing an identified referent.

  • C'est un médecin. — He/she is a doctor; identifying a person
  • Il est médecin. Il est gentil. — He is a doctor. He is kind; bare profession/adjective
  • Il y a un médecin ici. — There is a doctor here
  • Ce sont mes amis. — These/they are my friends
  • voici / voilà — here is/are / there is/are: presenting or pointing out
C'est ma sœur. Elle est médecin. Il y a deux patients.
This is my sister. She is a doctor. There are two patients.
il y a does not agree with the following noun’s number. C’est + adjective can comment on a whole idea; not every adjective requires il est.

Object pronouns

Identify the complement before replacing it.

Him, her, it, them: direct objects

A2forms & use

A direct object is selected without a preposition in the French verb frame. Replace it with the matching pronoun before the verb it belongs to.

  • me / te / nous / vous — me / you [familiar] / us / you [polite/plural]
  • le / la / les — him or masculine it / her or feminine it / them
  • m' / t' / l' — elided forms before vowel or silent h
Je vois Léa. Je la vois. Je vais la voir.
I see Léa. I see her. I’m going to see her.
In a compound tense the pronoun precedes the auxiliary; before an infinitive it belongs to that infinitive. Positive commands put it after the verb.

To him/her/them: indirect objects

A2forms & use

With selected French verbs, à + a person becomes an indirect object pronoun. lui means to him or to her; leur means to them.

  • me / te / nous / vous — to me / you [familiar] / us / you [polite/plural]
  • lui / leur — to him/her / to them
  • parler à Léa → lui parler — speak to Léa → speak to her
  • téléphoner à mes amis → leur téléphoner — phone my friends → phone them
Je lui donne le livre. Je vais lui parler.
I give him/her the book. I’m going to speak to him/her.
Not every à + person becomes lui/leur: penser à elle keeps a stressed pronoun. Do not choose direct/indirect by English “to” alone.

Y: there or à + a thing

A2forms & use

Y can replace a place phrase or the à-complement of selected verbs when it refers to a thing or idea. It goes before its verb, except in positive commands.

  • Je vais à Paris → J’y vais. — I’m going to Paris → I’m going there
  • Je pense à ce problème → J’y pense. — I’m thinking about this problem → I’m thinking about it
  • Je vais y aller. — I’m going to go there
  • Vas-y. — Go there / go ahead
Tu vas chez Léa ? Oui, j’y vais.
Are you going to Léa’s place? Yes, I’m going there.
Y has no single English translation. For a person as the object of penser à, use à lui/elle; a place such as chez Léa can still become y.

En: some, how many, about it

A2forms & use

En replaces an amount introduced by a partitive/indefinite/quantity frame, or a de-complement of selected verbs. Keep an explicit number or measure.

  • Tu as des pommes ? J’en ai. — Do you have apples? I have some.
  • Tu as trois pommes ? J’en ai trois. — Do you have three apples? I have three [of them].
  • Je parle du projet → J’en parle. — I’m talking about the project → I’m talking about it
  • Je vais en prendre. — I’m going to take some
Il reste du pain ? Oui, il y en a.
Is there any bread left? Yes, there is some.
En is not every English “some”, nor every de phrase. For the personal complements taught here use de lui/elle; object-pronoun order and agreement need their own limits.

Two object pronouns: order

B1forms & use

Ordinary pre-verb order uses the sequence below. Choose a legal combination for the meaning, not one item from every group.

  • me/te/se/nous/vous → le/la/les → lui/leur → y → en — ordinary order before finite verb, auxiliary or the relevant infinitive
  • Elle me le donne. / Elle le lui donne. — She gives it to me. / She gives it to him/her.
  • Donne-le-moi. / Ne me le donne pas. — Give it to me. / Don’t give it to me.
Je vais le lui donner. Je ne le lui ai pas donné.
I’m going to give it to him/her. I didn’t give it to him/her.
Positive commands use a different order and hyphens: verb + le/la/les + moi/toi/lui/nous/vous/leur + y + en. Negative commands return to ordinary order.

Replacing a noun

Mine, this one, someone and several.

Mine, yours, his, hers

A2forms & use

Replace a noun with a possessive pronoun. Choose the owner and match the gender/number of the thing possessed; keep the article.

  • le mien / la mienne / les miens / les miennes — mine: masculine singular / feminine singular / masculine plural / feminine plural
  • le tien / le sien — yours [familiar] / his or hers; same feminine/plural pattern
  • le nôtre / le vôtre / le leur — ours / yours [polite/plural] / theirs; feminine la, plural les nôtres/vôtres/leurs
Ma clé est ici. Où est la tienne ?
My key is here. Where is yours? clé is feminine.
The form follows the replaced noun, not the owner’s gender. notre/votre without an accent are determiners; nôtre/vôtre are pronouns.

This one, that one, that idea

A2forms & use

Use demonstrative pronouns without repeating a noun. Celui/celle/ceux/celles match a specific noun; ça/cela can refer to a situation or idea.

  • celui / celle / ceux / celles — the/this/that one or ones: masculine singular / feminine singular / masculine plural / feminine plural
  • celui-ci / celle-là — this one [masculine] / that one [feminine]
  • ça / cela — that/this idea or situation; ça is common in conversation
Cette veste est chère. Je préfère celle-là.
This jacket is expensive. I prefer that one.
Ordinary celui/celle needs completion: -ci/-là, de + possessor, or a relative clause. Do not use bare celui as a universal equivalent of “this”.

Someone, each one, several

B1forms & use

An indefinite pronoun stands for a person or thing without identifying it exactly. Distinguish it from a determiner followed by a noun.

  • quelqu’un / quelque chose — someone / something; singular
  • chacun / chacune — each one; masculine / feminine singular
  • quelques-uns / quelques-unes — a few; masculine / feminine plural
  • plusieurs / certains / certaines — several / some [masculine] / some [feminine]
  • tout / tous / toutes — everything [singular] / all of them [masculine or feminine plural]
Plusieurs sont partis, mais chacun a laissé un message.
Several have left, but each one left a message.
chacun takes a singular verb. chaque needs a noun; chacun replaces it. Not every English “some” uses the same French form.

Place & movement

Learn the relationship in a phrase.

At, to, in, from — place

A1forms & use

Choose the French place frame, not a single English equivalent. à/de contract with le/les; cities and countries have different patterns.

  • à + le → au ; à + les → aux — to/at the masculine singular or plural place
  • de + le → du ; de + les → des — from/of the masculine singular or plural place
  • à Paris / de Paris — in/to Paris / from Paris; ordinary city frame
  • en France / au Canada / aux États-Unis — in/to France / Canada / the United States
  • chez Léa / dans la maison — at/to Léa’s place / inside the house
  • ici / là / là-bas — here / there / over there
Je vais au marché avec Léa. Je reviens du marché.
I’m going to the market with Léa. I’m coming back from the market.
No contraction with la/l’: à la gare, de l’hôtel. Country/island names have exceptions; learn the article and place frame together.

Time & duration

For, since, ago and by need different choices.

Since, for, ago, in — time

A1forms & use

First choose the time relationship: continuing duration, bounded duration, elapsed time or a future interval. English “for” is not one French preposition.

  • depuis deux ans — for two years / since two years ago; continuing at the reference point
  • pendant deux heures — for two hours; duration
  • il y a deux jours — two days ago
  • dans deux jours — in two days; future interval from now
  • en deux heures — in two hours; time taken to complete
  • à huit heures / en juin — at eight o’clock / in June
J’habite ici depuis deux ans. Je pars dans deux jours.
I’ve lived here for two years. I’m leaving in two days.
A situation still continuing now normally uses present + depuis. pendant can appear with different tenses; “for” does not automatically mean past.

Complement frames

Learn verbs with their required partners.

À, de — or no preposition?

A2forms & use

Learn a verb/adjective with its complement frame and meaning. English “to” or “about” cannot predict the French preposition.

  • commencer à travailler / arrêter de travailler — start working / stop working
  • penser à un problème / parler d’un problème — think about a problem / talk about a problem
  • vouloir partir / pouvoir venir — want to leave / be able to come; no preposition
  • content de venir / prêt à partir — glad to come / ready to leave
J’essaie de comprendre et je commence à répondre.
I’m trying to understand and I’m starting to answer.
The preposition is part of the selected construction. Some verbs have multiple valid frames or changed meanings; this is a finite set, not a translation algorithm.

Relative clauses

Link by grammatical role, not English word matching.

Qui, que, où: link two ideas

A2forms & use

A relative clause describes a noun. Choose qui for its missing subject, que for its missing direct object, and où for place/time.

  • la personne qui arrive — the person who is arriving; relative is subject
  • le livre que je lis — the book I’m reading; relative is direct object
  • la ville où je travaille — the town where I work
  • le jour où elle est arrivée — the day she arrived
Voici le livre qui explique la règle que tu cherches.
Here is the book that explains the rule you’re looking for.
Qui versus que is not a people/things distinction. French does not normally omit the object relative as English can. Que elides before a vowel.

Dont: a link that includes de

B1forms & use

Use dont when the relative clause needs a simple de-complement, including possession. Identify that de relationship before choosing the pronoun.

  • le livre dont j’ai besoin — the book I need; avoir besoin de
  • la personne dont je parle — the person I’m talking about; parler de
  • la voisine dont le fils arrive — the neighbour whose son is arriving; possession
C’est le projet dont nous parlons.
It’s the project we’re talking about.
Do not repeat de or a possessive already represented by dont. A complex preposition such as à côté de requires a lequel form instead of this shortcut.

Preposition + lequel: recognition

B1recognition

Recognize a relative introduced by a preposition. Lequel forms agree with the noun they refer to; keep the preposition before the relative.

  • lequel / laquelle / lesquels / lesquelles — which: masculine singular / feminine singular / masculine plural / feminine plural
  • sur lequel / avec laquelle — on which [masculine] / with which [feminine]
  • auquel / auxquels / auxquelles — à + lequel / lesquels / lesquelles; à laquelle stays separate
  • duquel / desquels / desquelles — de + lequel / lesquels / lesquelles; de laquelle stays separate
Voici la table sur laquelle je travaille.
Here is the table on which I work. laquelle agrees with feminine table.
Recognition only: understand the referent, relation and agreement. Full productive contraction/complex-preposition choices are deferred. Dont is normally used for a simple de-complement.

What…: ce qui, ce que, ce dont

B1forms & use

When no specific noun supplies an antecedent, use ce + the relative matching the missing role. These forms can also frame an emphasis with c’est.

  • ce qui change — what is changing; subject
  • ce que je cherche — what I’m looking for; direct object
  • ce dont j’ai besoin — what I need; de-complement
  • Ce que je veux, c’est une réponse. — What I want is an answer; emphasis
Ce qui compte, c’est ce que tu fais.
What matters is what you do.
Not every English “what” is ce que. Choose the subordinate role; direct questions have a different frame.

Conditions & suggestions

Likely, imagined and what-if situations.

If — or what if?

B1forms & use

For a possible condition, use si + present with a present/future/command result. For an imagined present/future situation, use si + imperfect with conditional result.

  • Si tu viens, je reste. — If you come, I’ll stay / I stay; context supplies the present result
  • Si tu viens, je resterai. — If you come, I will stay
  • Si tu peux, viens. — If you can, come
  • Si j’avais le temps, je viendrais. — If I had time, I would come
  • Et si on sortait ? — What if we went out? a suggestion
Si tu as faim, nous pouvons manger ici.
If you’re hungry, we can eat here.
In these conditional si clauses, use neither future nor conditional after si. This limit does not apply to si meaning “whether”. Past counterfactual/mixed systems are deferred.

Reporting what someone says

Present reporting frames only.

Report what someone says

B1forms & use

With a present reporting verb, use que for statements, si for yes/no questions and the question word for information questions. Adjust people and references to the reporting situation.

  • Elle dit qu’elle vient. — She says she’s coming; statement
  • Elle demande si tu viens. — She asks whether you’re coming; yes/no question
  • Elle demande où tu vas. — She asks where you’re going; ordinary indirect order
  • Elle te dit de venir. — She tells you to come; order/request frame with infinitive
Léa dit : « Je suis prête. » → Léa dit qu’elle est prête.
Léa says, “I’m ready.” → Léa says she is ready.
Present reporting frame only: full past-frame tense backshift is deferred. Remove est-ce que/inversion in ordinary indirect questions; pronouns depend on who is reporting.

Connecting ideas

Cause, result, purpose, contrast and sequence.

But, whereas, on the contrary

A1forms & use

Use mais for a contrast, alors que to connect contrasting clauses, and au contraire to oppose or correct an idea.

  • mais — but; coordinates a contrast
  • alors que — whereas / while, depending on context
  • au contraire — on the contrary; rejects or reverses an idea
Je préfère le train, alors que Léa préfère le bus.
I prefer the train, whereas Léa prefers the bus.
alors que can also express time (“while”); context decides. au contraire is not a neutral substitute for every English contrast linker.

To do — or so someone does

A1forms & use

Express an aim with pour + noun/infinitive. A separate expressed actor can use pour que/afin que + present subjunctive.

  • pour le voyage — for the trip; noun phrase
  • pour travailler / afin de travailler — to work / in order to work; infinitive
  • pour que tu comprennes / afin que tu comprennes — so you understand; finite subjunctive clause
Je parle lentement pour que tu comprennes.
I speak slowly so you can understand.
Keep the intended actor clear. Purpose infinitives normally share the main actor in these basic frames; que introduces its own subject and a subjunctive verb.

Because — or because of?

A2forms & use

Choose a cause clause or a cause noun phrase. Other cause links can signal a shared reason, an explanation or a favourable cause.

  • parce que + clause — because; gives a reason
  • à cause de + noun — because of; often an adverse cause
  • grâce à + noun — thanks to; favourable cause
  • puisque / comme / car — since [shared reason] / as [cause] / for-because [explanation]
  • en effet — indeed/in fact; supports or explains
Je reste ici parce qu’il pleut.
I’m staying here because it’s raining.
parce que introduces a finite clause; à cause de/grâce à introduce a noun phrase. C’est pourquoi introduces a result, not the cause itself.

So, therefore, that’s why

A1forms & use

A result linker presents the consequence of the preceding situation. It points from the reason to what follows.

  • alors / donc — so, then / therefore, so
  • c’est pour ça / c’est pourquoi / voilà pourquoi — that’s why; ça is conversational
  • par conséquent — consequently; more formal
Il pleut, donc nous restons ici.
It’s raining, so we’re staying here.
The same word may have another use: alors can sequence events, and et can simply add information. A linker is not a mechanical translation of English “so”.

Add, order & conclude

A1forms & use

Choose a link for addition, alternatives, continuation, chronological order or conclusion. A short connector inventory stays tied to a clear sentence frame.

  • et / ou — and / or
  • en plus / également / aussi — in addition / also / also
  • tout d’abord / puis / ensuite / après — first of all / then / next / afterwards
  • premièrement / deuxièmement — firstly / secondly
  • enfin / finalement / à la fin — finally [sequence] / ultimately / at the end
Tout d’abord, je réserve. Ensuite, je paie. Enfin, je pars.
First of all, I book. Then I pay. Finally, I leave.
Finally is not one fixed translation: finally reaching an outcome, the last step, and the end of a period differ. Encore can mean still/again/more in context.

Despite, although, yet

B1forms & use

A concession keeps a fact while opposing its expected result. Choose a noun phrase, a finite clause or an adverbial link.

  • malgré + noun — despite
  • bien que + subjunctive clause — although
  • cependant / pourtant — however / yet, nevertheless
  • quand même — all the same, despite that; often conversational
Bien qu’il soit fatigué, il travaille.
Although he is tired, he is working.
malgré takes a noun phrase in the model taught here; bien que takes subjunctive even about a fact. Do not turn malgré que into the default clause pattern.
